# ⭐ Morning Briefing — Texas & DFW AI Data Centers, Grid, Land Use
*(Latest updates through September 9, 2026)*

**Top Story:** White Rose Partners withdrew its $580 million Project Tomahawk data center from Prosper after a week of mounting opposition, closing the town's most-watched zoning fight the same day Galaxy Digital disclosed 4.2 GW of newly classified Texas data-center capacity and Lubbock's City Council voted to hold off on large-scale data centers of its own.

## 📰 Today's News

### 1. 🏳️ Prosper's Project Tomahawk Is Dead — Developer Withdraws Before the Council Vote

- White Rose Partners withdrew its application for Project Tomahawk ahead of the Sept. 8 Prosper Town Council meeting where the project was scheduled for a final vote, according to a town news release reported by Community Impact. The application is now officially closed, and the Town Council will not consider it.
- The withdrawal follows the Prosper Planning and Zoning Commission's unanimous 7-0 recommendation of denial on Sept. 1, after more than an hour of public opposition at that meeting, including from Prosper Mayor David Bristol, who cited a lack of clear state and local guidelines for data centers.
- "We don't want to go into communities where we're not wanted," said Jeff Eigenbrood, a managing partner at White Rose Partners, per Community Impact.
- The application had sought to rezone 37.3 acres at Parvin Road and FM 1385 — chosen for its proximity to a Brazos Electric Power Cooperative substation — from agricultural use to a 225,000-square-foot data center. A community group, Prosper Residents for Responsible Land Use, gathered more than 2,000 signatures opposing the project ahead of the council meeting.

**Today's Signal:** This is the cleanest "concrete outcome" this briefing has tracked in weeks — not a delay, not a tabled vote, but an application withdrawn and officially closed — and it lands the same day two other North Texas communities took their own stands, suggesting organized local opposition is starting to win outright rather than just slow things down.

### 2. ⚡ Galaxy Digital Discloses 4.2 GW of Newly Classified Texas Data-Center Capacity

- Galaxy Digital announced that five of its Texas data center projects received conditional ERCOT Batch Zero large-load classifications covering roughly 4.2 GW of gross power capacity, according to the company's Sept. 8 release via PR Newswire, corroborated by StreetInsider and Stock Titan.
- Helios I (800 MW) and Helios II (830 MW) at Galaxy's Helios campus are classified as Batch Zero Base Load — capacity approved back in January 2026, part of more than 1.6 GW already cleared at the site, with Helios II still on schedule to energize in 2028.
- Caspian (700 MW), Selene (900 MW) and a third Helios expansion (1,000 MW) are conditionally classified as Batch Zero Studied Load, meaning they remain subject to capacity allocation in ERCOT's ongoing system-wide study.
- Galaxy's total Texas data-center pipeline now exceeds 5.7 GW across four sites, including a new 500 MW campus, Merlin, in McGregor. The classifications follow the PUC's Aug. 20 good-cause exception allowing ERCOT to issue conditional classifications while its audit — expected to take several months — continues.

**Today's Signal:** One day after IREN's Sweetwater hub became the first individual project this briefing saw win a public Batch Zero call, Galaxy's disclosure is roughly double the size — a sign the classification process is now moving in bulk rather than one project at a time, with the real test still ahead once ERCOT's multi-month audit actually concludes.

### 3. 🗳️ Lubbock City Council Votes to Hold Off on Large-Scale Data Centers

- Lubbock City Council unanimously passed a non-binding resolution declaring the city's intent not to allow large-scale data center development within city limits until the council approves its own regulations, according to KCBD and EverythingLubbock/KLBK.
- The vote follows a citizen-led petition seeking an 18-month moratorium on high-energy, high-water-use data center development, which was validated and moved to the council after being announced in late August, per the Lubbock Avalanche-Journal.
- Lubbock is not currently considering any specific data-center proposal — city officials framed the resolution as a preemptive statement of intent rather than a response to a live project.

**Today's Signal:** Lubbock joins Fort Worth, which took its own first step toward a moratorium in August, as another Texas city choosing to write the rules before a developer shows up rather than after — the same "write the rules first" argument Prosper's mayor made this week, just adopted preemptively instead of in the middle of a fight.

### 4. 🦖 Hood County Postpones Comanche Circle Decision to October 13, as NRG Drops the Gas Plant That Would Have Powered It

- Hood County Commissioners Court met Sept. 8 to discuss and potentially act on several proposed data-center developments, including the closely watched Comanche Circle project, but voted after public comment to move the item to Oct. 13 to allow time for proper public-hearing advertising, according to NBC 5 Dallas-Fort Worth.
- Comanche Circle, backed by Florida-based Sailfish Investors, could eventually span 2,100 to 2,600 acres near Tolar and the Paluxy River, with as many as nine data-center campuses.
- Separately, NRG Energy withdrew its air-permit application for the proposed Tolar Power Center, a 1,200-megawatt gas plant that would have supplied electricity to Comanche Circle, after opponents submitted thousands of comments urging regulators to deny it.
- The postponement follows an earlier ruling this summer by a district judge against developers who had argued two Hood County data-center projects should have been automatically approved under a 30-day deadline rule; the judge found that rule applies to subdivision plats, not standalone commercial concept plans.

**Today's Signal:** A month's delay isn't a denial, but pairing it with NRG's withdrawal of the power plant Comanche Circle was counting on shows the project's two biggest pieces — the data centers and the generation to run them — are both stalled at the same time, not just one or the other.
