# ⭐ Morning Briefing — October 26, 2026

(Covering Saturday, October 24 – Monday, October 26, 2026)

## 📰 Today's News

### 1. 🏚️ Damon Residents Organize Against a Possible CleanSpark Data Center on the Brazos

- On Sunday, Oct. 25, landowners in Damon, in western Brazoria County, held a community meeting after noticing a company buying more than 400 acres along the Brazos River, ABC13 reported. "We got to fight. We got to stop it before it gets here," one resident said.
- Residents said the county district attorney's office held a meeting earlier this month and showed them a map of seven parcels that included solar panels and batteries. They read that as a sign a data center is coming. Resident Christine Clark said that could mean gas turbines and round-the-clock "noise, lights, vibration."
- Residents identified the buyer as CleanSpark, which calls itself "America's Bitcoin Miner." In January, CleanSpark announced a deal to acquire up to 447 acres in Brazoria County to position itself for "a large-scale data center project." The company had not responded to ABC13.
- Some speakers framed the fight as protecting Black family land and heritage that has been in their families for generations. ABC13 noted that Texas counties have few tools to stop projects in unincorporated areas.

**Today's Signal:** CleanSpark announced this land deal nine months ago, but residents say they only learned about it at a DA's office meeting this month. A bitcoin miner moving into AI hosting fits the builder pattern we track. And in unincorporated Brazoria County, as in most of rural Texas, organizing early is the main leverage residents have.

### 2. 🏙️ San Antonio Gets a Fourth Data Center Option Ahead of Thursday's Council Vote

- The San Antonio Report laid out what City Council will choose from at its Oct. 29 session. Every option keeps data centers in industrial, commercial or manufacturing zones and off the Edwards Aquifer Recharge Zone.
- The newest option came from the Zoning Commission on Tuesday, Oct. 20. It would allow data centers by right in general, heavy and mixed industrial districts if they're 350 feet from homes, set back 100 feet from their own property line and use downward-facing lighting. In business-park, light-industrial and commercial zones they'd need a specific use authorization. The commission also recommended letting older data centers finish phased expansions.
- It competes with staff's by-right plan (500-foot buffers), staff's case-by-case plan, and Mayor Gina Ortiz Jones's hybrid. Her version has 1,000-foot separation, enclosed backup generators, 45 days' notice before permitting and two public meetings, and still requires a council vote on each project. "We should not also be attractive as, frankly, being a cheap date," Jones said.
- On Oct. 14, Jones and at least five council members leaned toward case-by-case approval. Council members Misty Spears and Marc Whyte pushed back. A Saturday Express-News op-ed (headline/snippet only) urged council to "do right on data centers."

**Today's Signal:** The Zoning Commission's by-right option is the one developers will push for this week. But the mayor plus five council members already favor case-by-case review, so Thursday's real question is how many conditions get attached, not whether council keeps its vote.

### 3. 🌾 Sid Miller Says Republicans Will "Pay for It" at the Polls Over Data Centers

- Agriculture Commissioner Sid Miller told NewsNation on Sunday, Oct. 25, that Republicans could lose this election because of rapid data center growth, and criticized Gov. Greg Abbott for not taking a stronger stand. "Basically, we've just quit listening to the people, and we're gonna pay for it this election," he said, adding that "eighty percent of people don't want a data center in their backyard."
- MS NOW's weekend program ran a segment (headline/snippet only) titled "'Taking advantage of rural Texans': Data centers become focal point as Texas Gov. race heats up," as campaigns entered their final 10 days.
- CBS News (headline/snippet only) reported Sunday that the data center boom "puts GOP in a bind" before the midterms, pointing to growing public opposition despite President Trump's backing.

**Today's Signal:** Democrats have been making this argument for weeks. Now a statewide Republican official is making it on national TV, which makes it harder for Abbott to brush off the pause criticism as partisan. With early voting ending Friday, this is the last weekend that message could reach voters.

### 4. 🔌 Battery Developer Bimergen Applies to Sell Power Directly to Texas Data Centers

- Bimergen Energy (NYSE American: BESS) said Monday, Oct. 26, that its subsidiary Emergen Commodities applied to the PUC for an Option 2 retail electric provider certificate. That certificate lets a REP serve specifically identified customers contracting for at least 1 MW.
- The application was filed Oct. 5 under PUC Docket No. 60368 and is still under review. Bimergen says it's working with "multiple counterparties" to arrange credit support for large data center loads.
- The company, best known for battery storage, framed the move around what it called Texas' "$200 billion" data center pipeline. It has not named a customer.

**Today's Signal:** With interconnection on hold during the audit, some companies are trying to make money from data center demand without building anything. A battery developer applying to resell power to unnamed data centers is a small example. The credit support is the part to watch: a REP serving a few very large customers takes on a lot of risk if one of them fails to pay.

### 5. 💧 Fort Worth Water Will Require Closed-Loop Cooling for Data Centers

- Fort Worth Water Director Chris Harder told the city's water and wastewater capital-improvements advisory committee that the department has written a closed-loop requirement into its design standards for data centers. The Fort Worth Report's Documenters account, published Saturday, Oct. 24, covers the committee's Sept. 25 meeting.
- City estimates: evaporative cooling, which the city won't support, could average 4 million to 6 million gallons a month. Closed-loop systems would use about 300,000 to 1 million. A comparably sized neighborhood uses roughly 2 million.
- To keep developers from lowballing their numbers, Harder said each site gets a meter sized only to its loading study. Upgrading that meter could trigger impact fees, which are generally the developer's cost. Closed-loop discharge is expected to be hauled off by tanker, and the city wants the permit to record where it goes.

**Today's Signal:** Fort Worth is setting data center water limits through its utility's design standards, not a zoning ordinance. That approach gets around the debate over who should regulate first that split Tarrant officials last week. Sizing meters to the loading study is a smart guardrail: a developer that underestimates its water use pays to upgrade later.

### 6. 🏨 A Workforce-Housing Company Buys Land in Fort Stockton for Data Center Crews

- Austin-based Armadillo Hotel Group said Saturday, Oct. 24, that its Armadillo Workforce Housing division bought land next to Fort Stockton in Pecos County to house construction and engineering crews. The paid press release was distributed via EIN Presswire.
- The company tied the purchase to West Texas' data center buildout, naming the announced GW Ranch off-grid power and AI campus. It plans modular rooms with dining, laundry and housekeeping, similar to its camps elsewhere in the Permian.

**Today's Signal:** Workforce camps go up before the campus does. When a company that houses oilfield crews buys land next to a planned AI campus, it's betting that GW Ranch construction will need workers in Pecos County soon.

### 7. 🧾 Haskell's Mayor: Sales Tax Up 123%, but the Town Is Stretched

- In a Dallas Morning News commentary published Saturday, Oct. 24 (headline/snippet only), the mayor of Haskell called data centers "a mixed bag." The piece says sales-tax collections in the town rose about 123% while growth has strained local resources.

**Today's Signal:** Haskell's mayor puts the trade-off rural towns face in two numbers: a big tax gain and a strained town. Lawmakers will hear both sides of it when they write abatement rules in January.
