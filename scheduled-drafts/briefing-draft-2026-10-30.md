# ⭐ Morning Briefing — October 30, 2026

(Friday, October 30, 2026)

**Top Story:** San Antonio stopped letting data centers build by right Thursday. On a 9-2 vote, City Council made every new project come before it for a special permit and kept the ban on building over the Edwards Aquifer recharge zone.

## 📰 Today's News

### 1. 🏛️ San Antonio Ends By-Right Data Centers: Every New Project Now Needs a Council Vote

- San Antonio City Council voted 9-2 on Thursday, Oct. 29, to require a specific use authorization for every new data center before construction, TPR and the San Antonio Report reported. The vote came one year to the day after District 6 Councilman Ric Galvan asked staff to start drafting rules. Misty Spears (D9) and Marc Whyte (D10) voted no.
- Data centers are now limited to commercial and industrial zones, and council will weigh each one case by case, looking at distance from neighbors, noise, water and energy use, and lighting. They remain barred from the Edwards Aquifer recharge zone. In a separate unanimous vote, council added setback review for projects near the South Side's UNESCO World Heritage missions.
- The stricter options lost. Mayor Gina Ortiz Jones's package, with 1,000-foot setbacks, noise limits, water and energy disclosures and extra public notices, failed 5-6. The Zoning Commission's version, which would have allowed by-right building in heavy industrial areas with 350-foot buffers, failed 5-6 after Ivalis Meza Gonzalez switched her vote. Staff's by-right option wasn't adopted either.
- Whyte, who backed the zoning commission's version, warned that under the new system "the rules are not written down. They get made in this room, one vote at a time." Galvan said every proposal "must come to council."
- The city has about 40 data centers, 19 of them in Galvan's West Side district, SA Current reported. Stream Data Centers founder Anthony Bolner backed the Zoning Commission version, and developers and construction workers asked for predictable rules. Residents raised noise, property values and air quality. None of the four outlets said when the rules take effect or how pending projects will be handled.

**Today's Signal:** Texas's second-largest city chose a council vote on each project over any written standard. That gives neighbors a hearing every time. It also means each approval depends on who's on council that day, which is exactly what developers objected to. The Bexar County data center Jones toured Monday, about 650 feet from a playground, sits outside city limits, so these rules wouldn't reach it.

### 2. 📑 PUC Staff Drafts the Contract Every Large Load Would Sign in ERCOT, With Water Disclosure and Audit Terms

- PUC staff filed a draft Standardized Large Load Interconnection Agreement (SLLIA) in Project 59999 on Thursday, Oct. 29, and asked commissioners to approve it for publication in the Texas Register at the Nov. 5 open meeting (Item 40). Rules & Projects attorney Mackenzie Arthur signed the memo. The filing runs 100 pages.
- The proposal notice says the form "will serve as the standard agreement for all large loads interconnecting within the ERCOT region." It carries out SB 6's requirement that large loads help pay the cost of connecting them, and folds in the large-load interconnection standards adopted in Project 58481 plus the transmission cost-recovery rules, including "minimum billing demand," still pending in Project 58000.
- The draft sets performance obligations at each stage of interconnection and requires large loads to disclose water and energy use. It also spells out audit and verification steps if the commission or ERCOT orders an audit, reallocates a customer's transmission capacity if phased energization slips past a 24-month delay threshold, limits assignment, and requires compliance with the Lone Star Infrastructure Protection Act by all parties and their affiliates.
- Many of the site-control and water questions in the draft's customer disclosures are taken wholly or partly from the requests for information the PUC and ERCOT issued in Project 59142 (Item 73). Staff says the cross-references to those RFIs will be removed when the form is adopted, but the questions themselves stay in.
- Comments, and any request for a public hearing, are due Dec. 14, 2026. Staff notes the form will change to match whatever the commission finally adopts in Project 58000.

**Today's Signal:** This is how this fall's scrutiny becomes permanent. The water and site-control questions regulators are asking developers now would become standard terms in every large-load contract, and the 24-month clawback means a project that stalls loses its transmission capacity. Commissioners will take up publishing those terms Nov. 5, the same day staff wants them to decide how to hear Batch Zero appeals.

### 3. ⚡ Chevron Says the Pause Won't Delay Microsoft's Power. Bloomberg Says Other Developers Are Looking at Utah.

- Chevron CFO Eimear Bonner told Bloomberg on Friday, Oct. 30, the day Chevron reported third-quarter results, that the company is still working through permitting, engineering and financing for its West Texas power plant for Microsoft: "And we don't see any of those things impacting the overall schedule of first power in 2028." Bloomberg also reported she cited a ramp-up by 2031. FourWeekMBA wrote up the interview; Bloomberg's story is paywalled.
- The plant, Project Kilby, is a co-located gas plant of about 2.67 GW that would supply a Microsoft-operated data center under a 20-year power purchase agreement, according to Chevron's June 22 release. That release put a final investment decision "by the end of 2026." Chevron's Oct. 30 earnings release doesn't mention Kilby, Microsoft or data centers.
- No free source says whether Kilby needs a TCEQ air permit of the kind Abbott's Sept. 21 directive froze.
- In a separate exclusive Thursday, Oct. 29, Bloomberg reported that Abbott's efforts to slow data center construction "are starting to work." Developers have begun looking at Utah, Georgia and Iowa, according to people familiar with the matter, FourWeekMBA reported. The write-up doesn't name the companies or projects involved.

**Today's Signal:** The pause is sorting developers by how much they have to lose. A company that builds its own power plant and has a buyer under contract says it can wait out the audit. Developers that need Texas permits and a grid connection soon are the ones Bloomberg says are looking at other states. The ERCOT report, due Dec. 10, lands three weeks before the end-of-year target Chevron set for its final investment decision.

### 4. 📉 New Era Raises $64 Million for Its Permian Campus, and Its Stock Falls 14%

- Midland-based New Era Energy & Digital (NASDAQ: NUAI) agreed Thursday, Oct. 29, to sell 23,272,727 shares at $2.75 each in a private placement worth about $64 million, set to close today, Oct. 30. Northland Capital Markets is sole placement agent.
- The shares fell 14.41% that day, Investing.com reported. The money will repay recent borrowings under the company's Macquarie facility and fund working capital.
- CEO Charlie Nelson said the raise was meant "to strengthen our financial position in a challenging market and meet our near-term financing needs," so the company can keep negotiating leases for 757 MW across Phases 1 and 2 of Texas Critical Data Centers. The campus is a 493-acre Permian Basin site in Ector County that the company says can grow to 1.4 GW.
- Phase 1 has up to 207 MW of firm power contracted under a 20-year PPA, with delivery expected in the third quarter of 2027, Kalkine reported. An additional $200 million in Macquarie financing becomes available only after leases are signed.

**Today's Signal:** Selling discounted stock to pay down debt is what a developer without a tenant does when credit is tight. New Era has land, a power contract and permits. What it doesn't have yet is a signed lease. Its next $200 million depends on getting one.

### 5. 🗺️ NBC 5 Counts the Southern DFW Pipeline: About 40 More Data Centers Planned Around Red Oak, Lancaster and Wilmer

- NBC 5 Investigates, using Cleanview and Datacenter Map data, reported Thursday, Oct. 29, that Lancaster, Red Oak and Wilmer have about 10 operating data centers between them, with almost four times as many on the way. Lancaster and Wilmer are in southern Dallas County. Red Oak is just across the county line in Ellis County.
- By NBC 5's count, Red Oak has 9 operating and 14 in planning or under construction. Lancaster has 3 under construction and 11 more in the pipeline, and Wilmer has 11 planned alongside one existing site. Skybox is building a 100-acre campus in Lancaster, and Compass Data Centers is planning one near a Red Oak resident's home. Compass didn't respond to NBC 5.
- Cushman & Wakefield's John McWilliams said Dallas's market, measured by power consumed, is on pace to nearly double in three to five years. The firm ranks DFW the world's No. 1 primary data center market.
- Red Oak Mayor Mark Stanfill said "a data center is actually a better deal for a city than a giant new neighborhood." Resident Lacey Hammel said what worries her is "so much, so fast."

**Today's Signal:** The I-35E/I-45 corridor south of Dallas is where DFW's next wave is concentrated, and those three small cities are making their decisions one zoning case at a time. Their mayor is selling data centers on tax math. Neighbors worry mostly about the pace.

- Early voting ends today. Election Day is Tuesday, Nov. 3.

**Today's Signal:** A statewide Republican official is predicting his own party will lose votes over data centers, four days before the election. Whoever wins, January's Legislature takes up rules on water, incentives and who pays for the grid.
