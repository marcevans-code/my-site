## 📰 Today's News

### 1. 🚔 Texas Rangers Review Guadalupe County Judge's Dealings With a Proposed Data Center

- The Texas Rangers are reviewing allegations that Guadalupe County Judge Kyle Kutscher may have violated state law in his dealings with representatives of the proposed Palomino Alpha Data Center, the San Marcos Daily Record reported. DPS Staff Sgt. Dila Hidalgo confirmed the Rangers "received the request" and that it is under review, with nothing more to release.
- According to the Seguin Gazette's reporting, cited by the Daily Record, Sheriff Joshua Ray got information this summer and passed it to the county attorney, who sent it on to DPS. The core allegation is that Kutscher kept taking part in Palomino Alpha matters while holding a financial interest through property he and his family own. At a June utility board meeting, he spoke in favor of the project as a landowner with "a piece of property in this transaction."
- The same allegations appear in a civil suit filed in July by Save Our Springs Alliance attorney Bobby Levinski for the Data Center Action Coalition. The suit accuses the Commissioners Court of Open Meetings Act violations in its deals with two projects, Cloudburst and Palomino Alpha. Kutscher and the county declined comment, citing pending litigation.
- Palomino Alpha has been off the Crystal Clear Special Utility District's agenda since August. The developer asked to pause a nonstandard water agreement for at least 500,000 gallons a day, and its feasibility study has since expired. Director Joseph Benavides said he expects the project "to resurface after the elections."

**Today's Signal:** Most data center fights end in a zoning vote or a lawsuit. This one now has state police looking at it. When the official presiding over the court that grants abatements has land in the deal, every vote that court took becomes evidence. Watch other county judges who have signed data center agreements quietly check their own disclosure forms.

### 2. 📋 TCEQ Quietly Suspends 17 Data Center Stormwater Approvals as Watchdog Says OpenAI and Amazon Got Sign-Offs This Week

- Before TCEQ pulled its STEERS portal offline, 404 Media saw 17 data center-related permits in the agency's central registry switched from "active" to "suspended." All 17 were construction stormwater authorizations, which let crews grade and level a site.
- FracTracker Alliance researcher Sam Law told 404 Media that the portal kept issuing approvals after Abbott's pause, "including for OpenAI and Amazon this Monday and Tuesday." He warned that contractors used to the automated system may not know their coverage was suspended and could keep building at some of the state's largest hyperscale sites. TCEQ did not respond to 404 Media.
- Quartz (headline/snippet only) argued that Texas, like New York with fracking, has blocked data centers before studying them, and said developers could seek compensation if a pause with no end date becomes permanent.

**Today's Signal:** Suspending a permit on paper and stopping bulldozers are different things. With approvals reportedly going to two of the biggest names in AI as recently as Tuesday, TCEQ now has to show it can enforce the pause on active job sites, not just in its own database.

### 3. 📉 Audit Limbo Hits Texas Power Stocks: Vistra Falls 9.9%, NRG 5.7%

- Irving-based Vistra fell 9.9% Thursday, Oct. 22, closing near $140.64 after a $156.14 close the day before, on volume 141% above average, MarketBeat reported. Morgan Stanley trimmed its target by $1 to $226 and kept an Overweight rating, which on its own doesn't explain a move that size. MarketBeat listed the Texas data center review among the risks weighing on the stock.
- NRG fell 5.7% to $94.07 the same day. A MarketBeat analysis said both companies are waiting on the state's verdict. ERCOT is weighing more than 474 GW of connection requests, about 90% from data centers. NRG has disclosed nearly 300 MW of retail data center agreements at two Texas sites and a 1.2 GW bring-your-own-power project. Vistra hasn't disclosed specific Texas data center contracts and reports earnings in early November.
- The Houston Business Journal reported (teaser only) that CenterPoint collected nearly $900 million in security deposits from large-load customers that applied to Batch Zero, and that the money is being spent even while the audit is underway.

**Today's Signal:** Wall Street has priced in Texas data center demand for two years. A one-day drop of nearly 10% at the state's biggest generator shows how little investors trust that demand to arrive on time while Austin re-checks the queue. CenterPoint's $900 million shows developers have real money tied up in that delay.

### 4. 🗳️ Tribune: Data Centers Are Cracking the Rural Republican Vote, From Corsicana to Grimes County

- A Texas Tribune report published Friday finds rural voters angry about data centers and transmission lines considering Democrats in numbers not seen since the Obama era. Public polls since late September put Democratic governor nominee Gina Hinojosa at 28% to 38% of the rural vote. A late-September Fox News survey had Senate nominee James Talarico at 42% in rural Texas.
- In Corsicana, about an hour southeast of Dallas and home to the RIOT data center, longtime Republicans brought binders on noise, air quality and power bills to a Sept. 29 roundtable with Hinojosa. Michael Ellis, 70, who lives less than a mile from a new data center in Navarro County, called Abbott's early support "disqualifying" and said he worries the pause is election-year pandering.
- Former Republican state Rep. Byron Cook of Corsicana said he's backing Hinojosa. Abbott spokesman Eduardo Leal said the governor "will always put rural Texans first." In Grimes County, residents near SpaceX and Tesla's Terafab site are weighing a crossover vote in the 10th Congressional District, where the Republican nominee is Elon Musk's lawyer, Chris Gober.

**Today's Signal:** Navarro County is DFW's growth edge, not distant ranch country. If data centers can move lifelong Republicans there, the issue reaches beyond rural Texas into the outer suburbs both parties count on. Early voting has a week left.

### 5. 🔦 Fort Worth Neighbors Find a Google Server-and-Battery Warehouse Going Up Behind Their Homes

- Residents near Silver Creek Road in Fort Worth told NBC 5 they didn't know about a Google-related project until barricades went up and crews started clearing land. One neighbor described 12-hour workdays that have turned a quiet end-of-the-street neighborhood into "a war zone."
- The city says the site is a Google storage and logistics facility for servers and batteries that support data centers, and that, based on what it has been told, it is not itself a data center. A city spokesperson said the land appears to have been rezoned in 2021. Google and its developer didn't respond to NBC 5.
- Neighbors are asking for a construction timeline and for buffers against noise and light pollution.

**Today's Signal:** Because the land was rezoned five years ago and the facility is classified as a warehouse, the residents had no zoning hearing where they could object. The data center supply chain doesn't stop at the campus fence. It includes warehouses, chiller plants and substations, and each comes with its own neighbors.

### 6. 🏛️ Tarrant Officials Split on Whether Cities Should Regulate Data Centers Before Austin Does

- At the Fort Worth Report's Growth Summit on Thursday, Tarrant County Commissioner Matt Krause urged local governments to wait for the Legislature. He pointed to the state's 2014 lawsuit over Denton's fracking ban as a warning about cities getting ahead of the state. He said Tarrant has already asked lawmakers to end data center tax abatements, and he floated a regional body like the Regional Transportation Council for projects that span several cities.
- Fort Worth Council Member Elizabeth Beck said incentives for data centers should be off the table and that Fort Worth, as a home-rule city, should use its authority now. "I trust the state government like I trust gas station sushi," she said. She cited Westlake's Circle T proposal as a reason to change state law: the 300-foot mailed notice for zoning cases doesn't reach residents across a city line.
- Public Citizen Texas director Adrian Shelley said cities should regulate "up to the limits of their authority" and that local officials should never sign nondisclosure agreements.

**Today's Signal:** Two Tarrant officials from the same party, working on the same issue, disagreed openly about who should go first. Beck's cross-boundary notice fix is the most specific bill idea DFW has offered for January, and Westlake–Keller is the example lawmakers will hear about.

### 7. 💧 Gradiant Signs On to Supply Water for a Hyperscaler's West Texas Campus

- Water-treatment company Gradiant has agreed to build complete water and wastewater systems, including potable supply and treatment, for a hyperscale AI campus under construction in West Texas, the Midland Reporter-Telegram reported. The customer is described only as "one of the world's largest technology companies."
- CEO Prakash Govindan said the system is designed for zero discharge to surface water. He argued that closed-loop cooling mostly shifts water use to the power plants supplying the grid. He said Gradiant is working with or talking to all four major U.S. hyperscalers.
- Govindan said the state's push to make data centers report water use to the Texas Water Development Board means water planning will happen "from the start of a project."

**Today's Signal:** Gas pipelines are being built to supply West Texas sites, and now water systems are too. An unnamed hyperscaler is building out its own fuel and water supply in the Permian, which is less a data center campus than a small industrial town.

### 8. ⚡ Landowners Say the Howard-to-Solstice 765-kV Route Would Cross a San Antonio Park

- Texas Scorecard reported that the Sabinal and Frio Rivers Alliance's Oct. 13 rehearing motion asks the PUC to drop Route 4 Mod 1 for the AEP Texas–CPS Energy line in favor of Route 23. The motion argues that the approved route crosses the city-owned Medina River Natural Area without the notice, hearing and findings required by Chapter 26 of the Parks and Wildlife Code.
- The alliance also says the route crosses land held by entities tied to Sun Guangxin, which it argues raises Lone Star Infrastructure Protection Act concerns. Those companies have called claims that they act for the Chinese government "categorically false." The motion hints at a court appeal if the PUC doesn't act.

**Today's Signal:** The parks argument is new, and it's specific. It's a procedural claim a court can check, which gives landowners a stronger hand than simply arguing about cost.
