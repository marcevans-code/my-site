# ⭐ Morning Briefing — Texas & DFW AI Data Centers, Grid, Land Use

**(Covering Saturday, September 19 – Monday, September 21, 2026)**

**Top Story:** A national CBS "60 Minutes" segment turned Ellis and Hood counties into the face of America's data-center backlash this weekend — and revealed Amazon Web Services quietly walked away from a Hood County site after commissioners balked at a 90% property-tax-rebate ask.

## 📰 Today's News

### 1. 📺 "60 Minutes" Puts Red Oak and Hood County at the Center of the National Data-Center Backlash

- CBS's "60 Minutes" aired a nine-and-a-half-minute segment Sunday night, "The data center uproar," built almost entirely around North Texas. Correspondent Norah O'Donnell profiled Red Oak (Ellis County) resident Jazmin Villegas, a Marine veteran and mother of five fighting Compass Datacenters' plan to build 10 two-story data centers on farmland near her home — one of seven Compass campuses that, if completed, would cover more than a quarter of Red Oak's roughly 20,000-person footprint. Neighbor Dave Lowe, a self-described lifelong conservative Republican, told CBS the issue has him planning to cross party lines in November: "I'm not going to vote red or blue; I'm going to vote for the candidate that is willing to speak up."
- The segment's sharpest disclosure came from Hood County, 70 miles west, where commissioner Dave Eagle described an unnamed developer — later revealed as Amazon Web Services — pitching the county on a project under codenames like "Project Yellow" and "Project Lion" while asking for a 90% property-tax rebate over 30 years. Eagle said no. AWS confirmed to CBS it "ultimately decided to not build in Hood County, and we received no incentives from the county," and that the project is now moving forward in a neighboring county that did grant the tax break.
- Gov. Greg Abbott's office, in a statement to CBS, reiterated that "data centers will not connect to the Texas grid until the ERCOT audit is complete," listing water, power, cost and neighborhood-disruption standards any project must meet first. Compass Datacenters defended its Ellis County record, citing five years of community engagement and closed-loop water systems.

**Today's Signal:** This is the first time this fight has been the subject of a marquee national news segment rather than local coverage — and the detail that will travel is concrete and checkable: a Fortune 50 company asked a rural Texas county for a 90% tax giveaway, got turned down, and left. That's a very different story than "residents are worried."

### 2. 🗳️ Data Centers Become a Defined Fault Line in the Texas Governor's Race

- KXAN Austin reported Sunday that Democratic state Rep. Gina Hinojosa, running against Abbott, spent the summer touring rural communities — including Granbury in Hood County — and has built a five-point data-center platform: require data centers to hire union labor, forgo NDAs with local governments, prove electricity and water costs won't rise for residents, end tax subsidies, and make water contracts unenforceable if they threaten a community's supply. She's calling on Abbott to issue a moratorium until the Legislature convenes in January.
- Abbott's campaign pushed back by citing Hinojosa's own voting record: her 2017 vote for HB 4038 and 2023 vote for HB 3104, both of which touched data-center tax-incentive rules. "Gina Hinojosa's accusations are a confession," said Abbott campaign press secretary Eduardo Leal. Hinojosa's camp counters that Abbott signed the original 20-year sales-tax exemption for data centers (HB 2712) in 2015 and, as recently as last November, was courting Google's $40 billion Texas investment as proof Texas is the "epicenter of AI development."
- Both campaigns are now actively relitigating years-old legislative votes in real time — a sign the issue has moved from regulatory dispute to campaign messaging with six weeks until early voting.

**Today's Signal:** When both a sitting governor's campaign and his opponent's are trading specific bill numbers from 2015, 2017 and 2023, the data-center fight has stopped being a side issue and become a scored campaign vulnerability for both sides.

### 3. 🏗️ D-FW Keeps Expanding Even as the Backlash Grows — By the Numbers

- The Dallas Morning News published a data-heavy look Saturday at just how large North Texas's data-center footprint already is. CBRE, which tracks the market closely, counts roughly 60 leased data-center buildings in D-FW totaling about 1.4 gigawatts of capacity — enough to power roughly a million homes. US Data Center Map's tracker counts 143 operational facilities in D-FW (60 in Dallas, 28 in Fort Worth, 22 in Plano) with 2.4 GW of combined capacity, and puts Texas statewide at 349 operating sites and 10.6 GW — second nationally only to Northern Virginia.
- The pipeline is bigger than what's built: US Data Center Map counts 29 D-FW projects under construction and 44 more planned, and 101 under construction statewide with 156 planned, putting Texas's incoming capacity pipeline at roughly 85 GW — higher than any other state's. Commercial real-estate firm Cushman & Wakefield recently ranked greater Dallas the top primary data-center market in the world, with Austin/San Antonio ranked the top secondary market and West Texas the top tertiary market.
- "Within five years, I expect D-FW to be the largest data center market in the world," CBRE's Brant Bernet told the paper — contingent on the region "bridging the power gap," a direct reference to the same ERCOT interconnection bottleneck driving Gov. Abbott's ongoing grid-connection audit.

**Today's Signal:** The backlash and the buildout are running on parallel tracks, not opposing ones — the same weekend "60 Minutes" aired a national segment on Texans fighting these projects, industry data confirmed the region's pipeline is still accelerating toward becoming the largest data-center market on Earth.

### 4. 🏛️ State Senator Previews a Crowded Legislative Docket on Data Centers

- State Sen. Charles Perry (R-Lubbock), who chairs the Senate Committee on Water, Agriculture and Rural Affairs and represents a West Texas district that includes active data-center sites near Abilene and Childress, said Monday he expects "hundreds" of data-center-related bills when the Legislature convenes, largely codifying practices he says the industry has already adopted voluntarily.
- Perry, discussing a recent committee hearing on data centers' water use, said Texas consumes about 17 million acre-feet of water a year statewide against an estimated 4,000 acre-feet currently used by data centers, and pointed to newer cooling and water-reuse technology as reducing that draw further. On power, he noted Senate Bill 6's 75-megawatt threshold now governs which large-load interconnections face stricter PUC review, and confirmed TCEQ — not the PUC or ERCOT — retains permitting authority over emissions from on-site gas generation projects like Crusoe's proposed plant.
- Perry also flagged a less-discussed local impact: in Abilene, he said, construction-worker housing demand has made rent "probably the number one issue I hear about" from residents, separate from the water and grid fights dominating DFW-area coverage.

**Today's Signal:** Perry's water math — 4,000 acre-feet of data-center use against 17 million statewide — is the kind of specific, quotable number opponents and proponents will both start citing; whoever uses it first in the next legislative fight will likely be quoting a state senator, not an advocacy group.

TCEQ permitting activity beyond the ongoing Knox Ranch (Hood County) public-comment window stayed quiet this weekend, and no new insurance- or stock-market moves tied to a specific named Texas project surfaced either.
